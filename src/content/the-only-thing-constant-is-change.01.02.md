So, if I find myself tangled in this messy yarn that has accumulated throughout my life, a good place to start figuring a way out would be to ask myself: "Is this the real life, or is it just fantasy?"

The line from the song is playful, but the paraphrased version behind it is deadly serious: _What can I be absolutely sure of?_

The reason this inquiry is so necessary is that it attempts to get down to the absolute foundation of the structure I call understanding. It requires courage, because it is easy to dismiss it as a road to insanity and confusion—a fear that is not entirely unfounded. Yet this road of inquiry is completely unavoidable; sooner or later, life delivers a collision that forces us to tread it. I am not going to defend this point of view as an academic thesis; I am simply going to outline my personal discoveries along this path.

When the question of absolute certainty—of absolute reality—is posed, it becomes evident almost immediately that firm ground is exceedingly hard to find. How do I know that something around me actually exists? I can reach out and feel objects with a variety of senses. But the problem is that I experience those exact same vivid sensory sensations when I sleep, and upon waking, I am always surprised by how thoroughly convinced I was that the dream reality was real.

By attempting to distinguish waking reality from dreams, however, a crucial constant emerges: the "I" that experiences both states. Across the shifting scenery of waking life and the hallucinations of sleep, something remains identical—me.

So, knowing that there are experiences and there is an "I," I can ask myself: how do I know that I exist? Attempting to answer this, I ask whether it is even possible to doubt my own existence. The moment I try to doubt it, I realize that there must still be someone doing the doubting. I cannot get rid of the doubter while in the very act of doubting everything.

My analytical mind sees this self-awareness as an undeniable anchor. My heart and my emotions, on the other hand, might occasionally protest out of sheer exhaustion, tempting me to throw my hands up and trust nothing at all. But if I were to follow my heart down that path, I would fall into the trap of ultimate skepticism—the most irrational, impotent, and useless form of philosophizing.

The knowledge of personal existence stands as genuine, unshakeable knowledge; it possesses the certainty that my mind desires. But what about the experience part? Do I know with the same certainty that the external world exists?

Ultimately, I know nothing about the world apart from what is presented to me directly through experience. I am aware that inside me lies a strong conviction that an external world exists, but this conviction does not possess the same logical certainty that the existence of the self has.

The realities of dreams and waking life feel remarkably similar in structure. Both have strict limitations regarding what I can control. Even if I can manipulate parts of my environment through physical action in waking life, or through lucid dreaming in my sleep, these realities are, for the most part, not something I fully command; rather, they command me.

Upon further examination, I see that my mind relies on specific internal faculties to distinguish between what is real and what is not.

My memory tells me a story of what has been and convinces me that there is a past.

My imagination allows me to preview what could happen and even experience those scenarios mentally. Imagination functions like a controllable, low-resolution simulator of reality, though its sensations are far weaker compared to the vivid stream that presents itself to the mind as waking life.

My senses press upon me with pain, fear, and hunger—sensations that present themselves to the mind with overwhelming intensity. Indeed, the senses force me to believe reality is real because my soul desperately wants to avoid the experience of fear and pain.

Yet, despite that intensity, sensory force still offers no logical guarantee that experience will not eventually dissolve like a dream. If I cannot determine whether experiences are rooted in an external world or not, can I somehow step out of this strange cinema to inspect the projector? That I do not know. But even if it were possible, wouldn't that just be moving into a different cinema room? The "I" and the stream of experience are essential, bound components of what I call reality; take one away, and the other ceases to make sense.

What does this realization give me? It gives me a way of setting a reference frame. Previously, I assumed without question that the material world "out there" was the ultimate, primary reality. But through this simple introspection, I am no longer so sure, and it becomes far more valid to shift my reference frame entirely—declaring that the only substance, the only thing my mind gives me as an absolute certainty, is the existence of the mind itself.

In this chapter, I want to emphasize that this exact type of introspection is what true philosophy has always been about.

However, to understand how philosophy can be useful for our lives, we must make a necessary remark: it is quite funny that philosophers as a group cannot even agree on what philosophy is, let alone agree on any truth they claim to discover. But we should not let this be a deterrent, as my own experience learning about philosophy has taught me a few crucial lessons.

First, I recognize that academic philosophy operates in a threefold structure: it consists of a community, a system, and a method. It behaves essentially like a sport, a board game, or a corporate software framework. People learn the rules and operate within their established boundaries. Over time, it becomes a self-preserving tradition—like soccer or basketball—where breaking changes are actively rejected because the institutional system is perfectly content on its own.

As part of this communal activity, the academic method focuses almost entirely on minor clarifications rather than the discovery of fundamental truth. It dictates the strict form an argument must take within a given system.

In Marxism, everything revolves around class struggle, so any new phenomenon must be reinterpreted to fit class struggle. In the Hegelian system, everything revolves around the dialectical process. In the Kantian system, everything must be mapped onto the distinction between _a priori_ and _a posteriori_.

The fundamental problem with academic philosophy is that academics do not necessarily look for the Truth; they simply maintain their chosen system. They build load-bearing intellectual abstractions that defend the pattern rather than checking whether the pattern corresponds to perceivable reality.

When setting out to understand my foundation, I want to be certain of the real state of affairs—I am after the Truth itself. I do not care how consistent or prestigious an academic system is if it is built on unexamined ground.

In this sense, I have little use for academic philosophy. Yet, I must admit it serves a purpose. Academic philosophy allows for comparative analysis. It introduces us to the arguments of great thinkers, serving as a whetstone to sharpen our own minds. But academic philosophy once fooled me into believing that philosophy is linear and progressive—that modern thinkers automatically possess a truer understanding than those of the past. That is a baseless assumption. Thinkers in the past often possessed a far clearer, deeper introspective grasp of reality than modern academics can hope for.

Even when academic philosophy is useful, its value is obscured because it so often reads like ciphertext—opaque, convoluted, and unapproachable. Why does a discipline dealing with the most fundamental, simple truths of existence become so painful to read?

This is best understood from a software development perspective. Programming is a straightforward activity where we build conceptual models, relying on machines to verify our logic. But when code is written inside complex enterprise systems, developers rely on unstated conventions, heavy tooling, and deep contextual metadata. To an outsider missing that context, reading the codebase feels impossible. The same thing happens in academic philosophy: philosophers wrote within dense, localized contexts, choosing words that served as obscure allusions rather than clear, direct descriptions of human experience. Their abstractions became load-bearing, and load-bearing abstractions resist clarity.

Unlike academic bureaucracy, true philosophy is deeply personal. It is an uncompromising examination of the mind. As Socrates declared in Plato's _Apology_, the unexamined life is not worth living. Socrates persisted in questioning assumptions even when it turned his entire society against him. While his dialogues frequently ended in _aporia_—an unresolved contradiction or loose thread—that incompleteness was not a failure; it was the exact fuel required to build a deeper, cleaner understanding.

When I philosophize, I am building an internal system. If I fail to construct a coherent framework over the course of my life, my understanding will remain fragmented, chaotic, and easily manipulated. But whereas academic philosophy builds systems for the sake of system preservation, a true philosopher grounds every abstraction strictly in direct experience, using philosophy as a living method to refine their code over time.

We develop tools for this personal method: constructing mental ontology trees, subjecting our beliefs to the court of critical doubt, drawing structural analogies, and isolating generalized algorithms of thought.

Yet, true philosophers are destined to feel somewhat lonely in their pursuit. The understanding we construct must remain soft—always subject to refactoring and complete revision when new truths expose a flaw in our logic. Academia and society, by contrast, desperately guard their rigid constructs against change, mistaking static dogma for strength.

At its core, true philosophy is always about distinguishing truth from mere appearance. This makes true philosophy fundamentally an epistemological project—the study of knowledge itself.

Even when we turn to ethics (how we ought to act) or lifestyle (how we ought to live), we are still solving an epistemological problem: what is real versus what is just a deceptive appearance? For example, acting unjustly toward others might yield short-term material rewards, but the underlying truth is that it fractures your character and destroys your peace of mind. Eating junk food offers the immediate appearance of pleasure, but the long-term truth is physical degradation. Epistemology is the discipline of stripping away immediate appearances to align our lives with truth.

Among historical thinkers, Immanuel Kant went the farthest in mapping this epistemic boundary. He brilliantly demonstrated that our knowledge is strictly bounded: we have _a priori_ structures hardwired into our minds (like logic and geometry), and we have _a posteriori_ sensory impressions that arrive through experience. Kant proved that empirical observations of the world can never give us absolute, universal truth, because any finite collection of experiences always leaves us asking, "What comes next?"

Yet, Kant made a critical mistake. He posited a mysterious, unknowable _thing-in-itself_—a "noumenon" hiding behind our senses that we can never perceive or know, yet upon which his entire system depended. He spent hundreds of pages warning us against metaphysical excess, only to smuggle an unprovable ghost through the back door.

A true philosopher must have the courage to go further than Kant's hesitation. We must admit that our experience has boundaries without inventing unknowable ghosts behind the curtain. Relative knowledge derived from experience remains immensely useful, but we must be honest about what constitutes our actual foundation.

This brings me to the fundamental decision of my entire worldview. I must choose my reference frame. Do I continue using the "outside" world—a world of unprovable, dead matter—as my primary reference frame? Or do I shift my reference frame entirely to the only thing my existence gives me with absolute certainty: the mind and the ideas presented to it?
