In our hyper-focused, goal-oriented lives, it is incredibly easy to forget the one thing that is truly important. I used to think that thing was "meaning." But meaning is just an intellectual narrative label we place on things to feel like our life has a direction. Behind that abstract notion hides something much more concrete, much more valuable, and much more real: love—the Christian kind.

To understand why love is more concrete than meaning, we have to look at how we process reality. I live mostly in my brain. My worldview is highly analytical, treating everything as a problem to solve. But this analytical approach has been deeply infected by the modern worldview, which is built on a very specific, unquestioned set of axioms.

## The Fragile Axioms of the Modern Mind

If we trace modern thinking back to its roots, we find it relies on three fundamental premises:

1. **Matter is the fundamental reality.** The physical, unthinking "thing-in-itself" is the baseline of the universe.
2. **Consciousness is an emergent byproduct.** The soul, the mind, and human feelings are just secondary illusions created by chemical reactions and biological computers.
3. **The universe is an equation.** Everything can and should be solved through detached, mechanical logic.

Because we accept these axioms, we reach conclusions that seem highly logical but are actually incredibly fragile. We start treating God as just another equation to solve. We reduce human consciousness to absurdities—casually concluding that we are just "agents collapsing wave functions."

When we operate on these premises, the mind crowns itself as God. But the mind was never meant to be a ruler; it is our intellectual immune system, designed to protect us from false things. When an immune system overacts, it destroys the body. When the analytical mind overacts, it falls into the "hard problem" of consciousness and spirals into postmodernism and deconstructionism—using logic to produce mistakes and ultimately destroying the validity of the mind itself.

## The Axiom Swap: Losing Nothing, Gaining Everything

We can escape this trap by simply swapping our foundational axioms. Immanuel Kant saw the limits of pure reason, but he made the cardinal sin of clinging to that hidden material "thing-in-itself," which fractured his philosophy.

George Berkeley was far more honest. He pointed out a profound truth: we only ever know things through our perception. We have no way to verify a hidden material world existing out there in the dark. We can replace the fragile modern premises with Berkeleyan axioms:

1. **Consciousness (the soul) is primary.** Epistemology—how we actually experience and know things—comes before everything else.
2. **The universe is mind-like.** The world is sustained not by dead matter, but as the true content of a Greater Mind.

If we make this swap, we lose absolutely nothing. The world remains completely regular. Science still works perfectly. When we explore deep mathematical frameworks—whether Euclidean geometry or the intricate, recursive distributions of prime numbers—we aren't just uncovering random material coincidences; we are reading the structured, orderly thoughts of the Mind that sustains the world.

But while we lose nothing, we gain everything. In this framework, souls are the first-class citizens of reality.

## The True Field of Battle

This changes everything about how we view love. As Father Seraphim Rose warned, Christians are highly vulnerable to a naive, superficial idealism because we secretly try to play on the materialist's home field. If we accept that matter is primary, then Christian love looks like a fragile, childish sentiment, easily dismissed by skeptics.

But when we realize that the soul is primary, love ceases to be a fluffy chemical reaction. It becomes the fundamental nourishment of reality—a multi-layered fulfillment that hits your feelings, your need for connection, and your need for safety all at once. Analytical skills are almost the enemy of this, because if you treat love as an analytical problem, you miss the point entirely.

Our souls aren't just the intellect; they are made up of imagination, memory, and, at the very center, the heart. It is easy to dismiss feelings because they can cloud our strategic goals (intelligence is often about delayed gratification, whereas a lack of it is pure directness). But feelings are not what is meant by love anyway.

## Dropping the Steering Wheel

In his book _The Four Loves_, C.S. Lewis gives a simplified but illuminating analysis of this. We are incredibly needy creatures. We rarely do anything out of pure love; we usually do things out of need. "Gift-love"—giving completely without needing anything back—is a quality that belongs uniquely to God. Yet, there is nothing that satisfies our human souls more than this type of love.

This love is humanly impossible. It is only possible for God, through Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit.

Jesus put things straight: stop trying so hard to imitate righteousness. When we try to force an imitation of being holy, we just become miserable Pharisees and make the world a worse place. We have to drop our analytical pride, break the ego of the intellect, and start with total honesty: _“I am a needy creature. On my own, I am completely incapable of Godly love.”_

Then we ask God for the only thing that matters: to let that love be present in our lives.

Will it magically appear? No. But we must consciously analyze and seek out opportunities to practice it. This practice isn't about mastering a skill in the traditional sense. We don't get "better" at love the way we get better at math. Instead, we get better at putting our faith in Christ and relying on Him to do the work. The "skill" is learning how to let go of the steering wheel.

Even when an act of love feels forced or fake because of our cold, analytical habits, it doesn't mean it is valueless. There is no more important work in life than this. If, by the end of your life, you realize that you sincerely attempted to ask God to help you love your neighbor and love Him, you will know your life was not in vain.

This world was never a problem to be solved. It was always a canvas for bringing Heaven closer to earth. In our goal-oriented lives, let's remember that every single goal is subservient to love—the true kind, the Christian kind. Let’s look past the abstract anxieties of righteousness, heaven, and hell, and keep our eyes strictly on Jesus Christ as the source of the true light.
