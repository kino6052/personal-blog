Having established mind as my primary reference frame, I can now step forward to examine the phenomenal world—the continuous stream of experience.

The most basic observation we can make about the world, as thinkers like Immanuel Kant and George Berkeley recognized, is that experience unfolds in space and time. Space and time are inseparable conditions of perception; we cannot imagine perceiving a physical object outside of spatial dimensions or temporal succession.

Yet, upon deeper reflection, we discover that space and time are not static background boxes waiting to be filled. They are relational frameworks. We become aware of time only because states shift relative to one another—an apple hangs on a branch, and later the same apple rests on the ground. We make a connection between the memory of the hanging apple and the perception of the fallen apple, recognizing them as the same entity across two different moments and coordinates.

This makes **change** even more fundamental than space and time. We only come to know space and time _through_ the observation of change. Even spatial dimension itself requires change to be understood: while a static view might give us a flat two-dimensional impression, understanding three-dimensional space requires movement—a change of perspective, walking around an object, or shifting our point of view. Space and time are relational acts of mind, and without change, there are no relations to measure.

## The Hegelian Illusion and the Rules of the Academic Game

In the history of philosophy, Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel is the figure most famous for placing change at the very center of his system. Yet, despite the major role change plays in the framework I am building, I explicitly reject Hegel’s method.

Recall our discussion from Chapter 2, where we noted that academic philosophy often functions as a threefold structure of community, system, and method—a self-contained game with strict rules. Hegel created one of the most captivating "game engines" in intellectual history. Scholars who learned the rules of Hegelian dialectics became intoxicated by the machinery, eagerly applying the triad of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis to every phenomenon they could find.

The fatal flaw of Hegel’s system is that it completely abandoned Kant’s anti-metaphysical guardrails. Kant taught us that philosophy must remain modest, setting strict limits on human knowledge and distinguishing between what is given in experience (_phenomena_) and unprovable speculative fantasies (_noumena_). Hegel ignored these boundaries. Blinded by his dialectical algorithm, he constructed an unfalsifiable, immodest fantasy: that history is the destined, logical evolution of reality toward a utopian Absolute Spirit.

We must be clear about the boundary between philosophy and science. A true philosophical foundation must be _modest_. Like Berkeley’s idealism, it should posit only the minimal necessary ground required for experience to make sense—in our case, the Sustainer Mind. Falsifiability does not belong at the level of foundational metaphysics; falsifiability belongs to the empirical models we build _on top_ of that foundation, testing and verifying them against direct experience, exactly as Kant prescribed.

Hegel made another critical error: because change is an attribute of the phenomenal world, it cannot be applied to _a priori_ knowledge. Mathematics and logic exist independently of sensory experience and are not subject to the physical decay or temporal shifts we observe around us. By conflating the changing phenomenal world with unchanging _a priori_ truths, Hegel blurred a vital line. Coupled with his notoriously dense, ciphertext prose that obscured rather than clarified, his system became an exercise in ungrounded speculative metaphysics.

While I reject Hegelian dialectics, the concept of change itself remains crucial. Stripped of grandiose utopian fantasies, change can be grounded in a rigorous, empirical framework.

## Rest, Conflict, and the Engine of Change

When we look at the phenomenal world, we must ask the fundamental question: _Why do things change at all? Why is the world not simply static?_

When we examine reality closely, a striking pattern emerges: **the phenomenal world constantly strives for rest, yet it is logically incapable of maintaining it permanently**.

Physical entities, in their default state, seek equilibrium. In their local reference frame, they tend toward rest. But because the world is inherently relational, static isolation is impossible. A moving entity crosses paths with a stationary one, attempting to occupy the same spatial coordinate. The two patterns interfere, resulting in an interaction: they bounce apart, stick together, or shatter into fragments.

Why did that interaction occur? Because a **conflict** arose. Conflict is not a physical object; it is a relational event—a moment where two patterns can no longer simultaneously maintain their state of rest. The resulting change is simply the system’s mechanism for resolving that conflict and establishing a new, temporary state of equilibrium.

This reveals the core axiom of the phenomenal world: **the world of experience is structured by an inevitable, recurring incompleteness**. Conflicts are bound to arise, and change is the universal process through which those conflicts are resolved.

In purely physical systems, conflict resolution is _greedy_. Physical objects take the immediate, path-of-least-resistance route to resolve tension. A falling rock does not pause to evaluate options; it takes the shortest vector to the ground. Carbon atoms bonding into complex molecules greedily seek lower energy states, even as the growing structure introduces new internal tensions. In physics, "energy" is fundamentally our bookkeeping label for persistent, unresolved imbalance passing through different forms.

## Intelligence as Deferred Resolution

This distinction between greedy physical resolution and indirect navigation provides the key to understanding **intelligence**.

While physical matter resolves tension immediately and blindly, intelligence is defined by how it approaches conflict. Intelligence recognizes that a direct, greedy push often leads to a worse collision or a suboptimal trap. Instead of seizing the immediate win, an intelligent agent voluntarily accepts a temporary detour—a short-term increase in effort or cost—to achieve a far deeper, more complete resolution later.

- A hungry predator does not charge directly across an open field; it accepts the detour of stalking from the treeline.

- A business does not demand immediate daily profit; it reinvests capital, accepting short-term costs to build long-term capability.

- A software engineer does not copy-paste quick hacks into a codebase; they accept the temporary friction of refactoring to keep the architecture soft and maintainable.

Intelligence is deferred conflict resolution—the capacity to navigate possibilities across time rather than reacting greedily to the immediate present.

## Asking "Why?": Causality as the Search for Necessity

This brings us to the human mind's relentless demand for explanation: the "Why?" question.

When we ask "Why did this happen?", we are not asking for a random narrative. We are asking for the **necessity** behind a change. In a world governed by change, asking "Why?" means asking: _What specific conflict arose that made this change necessary?_

Even when we look at an apparently static object—like a fossil embedded in rock—and ask "Why is it like this?", we are asking the exact same question. We are asking: _Why, out of all possible configurations, is it necessarily in this state? What past process, what sequence of environmental conflicts and resolutions, forced it into this specific form?_

This principle—that every change requires a conflict to necessitate it—functions as an _a priori_ requirement of human thought. Just as we know that two plus two equals four, our mind cannot process a change without assuming an underlying cause or tension that triggered it. To claim that something changed "for no reason at all" is an unprovable self-contradiction; it asserts an effect while denying the very relational tension that defines an effect.

Every cause is a conflict. Every effect is a resolution.
