Learning Chinese is like playing Donkey Kong.

The game is super hard. No saving points. If you lose, you have to start all the way back at the bottom. The barrels keep rolling down, and the boss at the top is absolutely brutal. But, with each restart, you understand a little bit more. You learn the speed of the barels, and you learn when to jump.

For the longest time, my rain simply rejected Chinese. I was the player who can't get past level one.

My first failure was at age 20. I checked out a book from the library. Hanzi, tones, no understanding what is going on, these barrels hit me before I even started moving. So I quit.

My second failure was in college. I wanted to impress some of my Chinese friends. So I tried an audio course for a couple of months. After two months, I tried talking to them in Chinese. The result was they didn't understand anything I tried to say, even something as simple as "I can speak Chinese". That was another barrel.

My third failure happened during the pandemic. I was taking online classes. I thought that I finally making progress. Until I tried to watch videos on xiaohongshu realizing that I don't understand even 1 percent of what they say. Another barrel. Back to the bottom.

But a real hard "Game Over" moment was when after 3 years of studying, I first time came to China - Harbin. THe humbling realization was - I couldn't read, couldn't speak and couldn't understand a single word.

People love to talk about how much time is required to master a skill. For example, "you need 10,000 hours" or something like that . But time is just a useless number. It doesn't tell you where the barrels are coming from or how to jump etc. Not giving you any helpful information about the journey and what difficulties there are.

Every failure leaves a clue. You don't have to just blindly repeat, you change your approach with the information you receive.

The information I collected made me realize that the traditional Chinese learning methods are flawed because they force you to do many things at once - reading, writing, listening and speaking - it is like being shown how to do jumping, climbing, doding etc without first mastering either of them. In software engineering we call this a lack of "separation of concerns"

So I decided to think like an engineer. I asked myself: to pass this level, what is the single most fundamental skill I need? What is the main bottleneck?

The answer is listening. Without listening the game is hard-stuck.

Step One was to separate the "sound" from the "meaning."

I trained myself to only listen to the sounds, without immediately trying to translate them in my head. It is like just focusing on jumping at the right time without trying to understand where you are going.

This actually proved to work pretty well - I noticed that when I practice focusing on sounds only - no translation - I am able to train my ears to capture a lot of information that is later much easier to turn into understanding. I've noticed I mishear a lot of sounds. So by correcting it, meaning was much easier to get.

Step two was realizing that CHinese caracters drain a lot of time without giving much return. In French and German, reading helps your speaking and listening. But in Chinese, reading and speaking run ont two different systems. One doesn't help much with the other

I used flashcard software every day to memorize characters. A year later, I could read! But it did absolutely nothing to help my listening or speaking. I had to isolate these bugs and solve them separately.

Right now, I’m working on a personal project to solve a massive problem in this game: the finish line in Chinese is just too far away. You can study for years and still not understand real, native content. HSK1 - nothing, HSK2 - still nothing, HSK3 - still far away, HSK4 - you think you reached somewhere but you didn't, HSK5 - you can consider yourself a beginner. Because these methods don't address the real problems and bottlenecks.

So, I'm designing two "bridge languages" that I call Haoshuode and Jiandanhua.

These aren't cheat codes. They are engineered, simplified versions of Chinese. I understand that it is exactly what is missing for Chinese learners - the right tools for language.

I'm working hard to tackle the bottlnecks. I still haven't overcome the listening bottleneck.

I still have a "Final Boss," and that is: answering the phone when a waimai guy calls. If I can finish a phone call one day—that will be my true victory screen.

I haven't beaten the game yet, but at least I'm starting to see the patterns.

If you are learning Chinese, don't just blindly rush forward. Think like an engineer. Find your bottlenecks, separate your concerns, and conquer them one level at a time.

Because Chinese is like an arcade game. I haven't reached the end, but at least now, I know exactly when to jump.
