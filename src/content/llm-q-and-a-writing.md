## This article was written using the very approach it describes—a back-and-forth Q&A session with an LLM, then refined into the piece you're reading now.

The Aha Moment
You know that feeling. You're staring at a blank page. You have a project, an idea, a vague sense of something important you want to say—but your thoughts are incomplete, scattered, half-formed. You try to force it. You outline. You freewrite. You make lists. But the more you push, the more the idea slips away.
Traditional writing asks you to think ahead. It demands that you already know your conclusion before you type your first sentence. That pressure, ironically, kills the very insights you're trying to capture.
There's another way.
It's simpler, more natural, and—dare I say—actually fun.

---

What Is the "Interview Approach"?
Instead of sitting down to "write an article," you sit down to have a conversation. You bring a vague topic. You bring curiosity. You bring permission to wander. The LLM brings questions—patient, intelligent, non-judging, infinite in its willingness to keep asking until you arrive at clarity.
You answer one question. Then another. Then another.
You don't plan ahead. You don't outline. You simply respond to what's in front of you, in real time, step by step.
This isn't about producing polished text. It's about discovering what you actually think.

---

Why This Works: Three Core Insights

1. Natural Exploration of Uncertainty Space
   When you work on a project, you often don't know where to start—or your ideas are incomplete. The traditional approach forces you to artificially impose structure from the outside, like drawing a map of a country you've never visited.
   The Q&A approach does the opposite. It lets you explore the space naturally. Each question acts as an "uncertainty reducer." A well-formed question collapses ambiguity faster than any amount of solo staring. You're not guessing what might be relevant—you're responding to targeted prompts that reveal your own thinking to you.
   The result? You build exactly the information you need, no more and no less. No filler. No forcing. Just clarity emerging organically.
2. Externalizing Assumptions and Stress-Testing
   One of the hidden obstacles in solo writing is unexamined assumptions. You write a sentence that seems true, only to realize three paragraphs later that it was built on shaky ground. You've already invested time and mental energy into a dead end.
   In the Q&A approach, those shaky foundations get exposed in the answer to a single question. The back-and-forth acts as an iterative feedback loop. Each answer gets stress-tested by the next question. You catch confusion early, while it's still cheap to fix.
   Better yet, the LLM mirrors back your half-formed intuitions and gives them names. It hands you vocabulary for what you already felt. That "naming moment" can unlock entire new dimensions of understanding you didn't know were there.
3. Fun, Less Laborious, and Reusable
   Writing this way is pleasurable. There's no grind. No forcing. No "writer's block" because you're never staring at a void—you're always reacting to something concrete.
   It's also less laborious. The cognitive load is distributed across time and across the conversation. You're not holding the entire structure in your head at once. You're just answering the question in front of you.
   And once you have that raw transcript—a collection of Q&A—you have something powerful: a complete inventory of your own thinking. It's not yet an article, but it's the necessary and sufficient material for one. From there, you can refine. You can rearrange. You can polish.
   Or you can have another Q&A session dedicated entirely to refinement. The process scales with your needs.

---

The Magic Ingredient: The LLM as Partner
Why use an LLM for this instead of a human interviewer or a journal?
Three reasons:
Infinite patience. A human interviewer gets tired, distracted, or bored. The LLM never does. It will ask the tenth question with the same curiosity as the first.
Non-judging. There is no social pressure. No fear of sounding stupid. You can be raw, half-baked, meandering—the LLM doesn't care. It only wants to help you untangle your own thoughts.
Speed and intelligence. The LLM processes language patterns faster than any human. It can find threads you missed, offer unexpected connections, and hand you the exact question you didn't know you needed to answer.
Does that intelligence ever feel like a pressure to "keep up"? Occasionally. But the moment the questions become repetitive or unstimulating, you simply take back the wheel. You redirect. You ignore. You pivot. The control is always yours.

---

From Conversation to Article
The final step is simple. You take your Q&A transcript—let's say 15 to 20 exchanges—and you treat it as raw material. Not as a final draft, but as a map of your thinking.
From there, you can:
· Pull out the strongest answers and organize them thematically· Use the questions as section headers or structural beats· Rewrite your answers in a more polished, third-person voice· Cut tangents, preserve the soul, and retrofit clarity
The transcript is insurance. It guarantees that nothing important got lost in the fog of composition.

---

An Invitation
The next time you're stuck—staring at a blank page with a half-baked idea and no clear path forward—try this.
Don't write. Don't outline. Just open a chat with an LLM and say:
"Ask me questions about [your topic]. One at a time. I'll stop you when I'm ready to write."
You might be surprised what comes out.
Because sometimes the smartest way to write isn't to write at all. Sometimes, it's to let yourself be interviewed.
