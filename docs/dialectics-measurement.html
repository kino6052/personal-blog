An Empirical Alternative to Dialectics: Measuring Conflict through Perceived Choices and Game Dynamics

1. The Core Flaw in Hegelian Dialectics

Hegelian dialectics makes a fundamental metaphysical mistake: it attempts to place change and conflict into the a priori realm of pure logic. This is an inherent contradiction. Logic deals with static, timeless relations, whereas change is intrinsically bound to time, experience, and the phenomenal world. By treating historical progression as a logical necessity rather than an empirical process, dialectics functions as a pseudo-scientific framework rather than an observational tool. The only valid point of agreement with the dialectical view is phenomenal: in the real, observable world, change and conflict do exist, and systems naturally drive toward a state of rest, equilibrium, or stasis.

2. Human Conflict as a Function of Perceived Choices

In non-human physical systems, forces resolve directly through physical laws (e.g., pressure differentials balancing out). Human systems are far more complex because they operate through perception.

  • Needs vs. Wants: Humans rarely seek stasis based on objective needs alone; they act on perceived wants, which are frequently flawed or out of sync with actual requirements.
  • Default to Stasis: If people perceive that their current set of standard options allows them to maintain their baseline or status quo, they will choose to do so. Conflict does not erupt randomly; it arises when people perceive that their completeness or security is actively being taken away.

3. Game Theory as a Tool (Exorcising "Ghosts" from the Math)

Mathematical frameworks—specifically Game Theory—offer a concrete tool to model these interactions, provided we use them purely as descriptive shorthands rather than metaphysical truths.

  • Where Game Theory Fits: Strategic interaction, payoff matrices, and decision trees allow us to formalize how individuals evaluate their available choices against competing actors to reach personal stasis.
  • Exorcising "Ghosts": Many mathematical models introduce "ghosts"—reified abstractions like infinitely rational agents, unobservable continuous utility functions, or fictitious equilibrium states that exist only on paper.
  • Grounding the Model: To remain empirically valid, game theory must be stripped of these ghosts. It must measure only finitely observable, subjectively perceived option sets. The math must reflect what people actually think their choices and payoffs are in a given reference frame, not an idealized calculation.

4. Quantifying the Critical Point (The Tipping Point)

By combining perceived choice sets with game-theoretic payoff matrices, human conflict can be empirically quantified without resorting to vague historical metaphors.

The Threshold of Revolution: A system hits its critical point—the point of no return—not simply when discontent is high, but when the perceived payoff of every standard option on an actor's decision tree drops below a critical baseline (such as survival or extreme suffering).

When standard behavior yields a perceived payoff lower than radical action, revolting becomes the dominant choice in the agent's mental matrix. Measuring conflict requires empirical investigation (e.g., structured inquiry or behavioral tracking) to map how individuals rate their real choices. When non-action yields a payoff below baseline survival, catastrophic change becomes inevitable.

5. The Myth of "Synthesis" vs. Destruction and Knowledge Survival

Hegel claims that conflict resolves through a mystical "synthesis" (Aufhebung) that preserves and elevates opposing ideas into a higher context. In the phenomenal world, this is simply false:

  • Resolution is Often Pure Destruction: Conflict resolution does not automatically expand context or create a harmonious blend; it frequently destroys existing structures outright.
  • Why "Spiral" Progress Happens: When societies rebuild after a collapse and appear to progress in a spiral-like fashion, it is not due to a dialectical law. It happens because destruction is rarely total. Leftover knowledge, artifacts, and lessons from the past survive, giving the next generation raw materials to build upon while pursuing the exact same goal: attaining a new state of stasis.

6. Post-Collapse Dynamics: The Opportunistic Power Grab

Once the critical point is crossed and the old structure collapses, the decision matrices of surviving actors immediately shift. The removal of checks and balances alters the perceived payoffs for key individuals:

  1. Power Seeking: Opportunistic actors evaluate their new option tree and recognize options that yield ultimate power with minimal perceived immediate consequences.
  2. The Drive for Control: Driven by the desire to secure their own position (their personal stasis), they naturally execute those choices. Without tracking these shifting choice sets at every stage, social systems endlessly cycle between threshold collapse and unchecked power grabs.

Conclusion

Conflict is not a metaphysical engine driving humanity toward an abstract ideal. It is an empirical interaction pattern driven by human perception and strategic decision-making under constraints. By discarding a priori dialectics, stripping mathematical game theory of its unobservable "ghosts," and focusing strictly on how actors evaluate their perceived options, we can measure how close a system is to its breaking point and understand how equilibrium is actually lost and restored.
