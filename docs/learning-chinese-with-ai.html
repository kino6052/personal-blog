Learning Chinese with AI

How to make the hardest language a little bit easier

Home Page

Most people learn Chinese in a classroom: endless writing drills, memorizing vocabulary lists, and listening to textbook dialogues you'll never actually use. With this approach it takes years before Chinese becomes useful at all.

The good news is that learning Chinese today is easier than it has ever been — because of the tools we now have.

Yes, these tools are crutches. But reaching a high level of Chinese without them is like climbing Mount Everest.

Why Chinese is so hard

For a long time I thought calling Chinese "one of the hardest languages" was an exaggeration. Now I think it's true.

The difficulty isn't that the language is alien, or that our brains aren't built for it. The real problem is that Chinese is really four separate skills — writing, reading, speaking, and listening — and they barely reinforce each other. In most languages, progress in one skill pulls the others along. In Chinese, you can pour effort into one and stay weak in the rest. Each skill becomes its own effort drainer: a lot of work for very little return (low ROI).

Writing characters by hand takes an enormous amount of time and gives back very little. You can spend years on it and still read slowly and speak poorly.

Reading is hard because written and spoken Chinese are almost two different languages. It gets worse if you wander into classical Chinese, where you can spend years on a form that ordinary people don't speak and sometimes can't even understand. You end up knowing words and expressions no one actually uses.

Speaking can drain effort too. If you learn to express your thoughts in your own improvised way, native speakers still won't understand you. The highest-return first step is pronunciation: getting your sounds as close to standard Putonghua as possible. Once you understand how the sounds are formed, everything else gets easier.

Listening is probably the most important skill and the one that pays off the most — yet it stays out of reach for students even years in.

These days there are many channels that make "comprehensible input": simplified, easy-to-follow Chinese. It's a good start. But real Chinese people don't talk like that, so you hit a plateau — you can follow the easy videos, but native content still loses you. Travel in China and you'll notice it right away: accents, slang, and the messy reality of how people actually speak all get in the way.

This is the heart of the problem: as long as native content stays out of reach, fluency stays out of reach too. All four skills, learned separately, stand between you and the real thing.

How to unblock native content

So the real question is: how do I get the highest return on my effort? Or, more practically — how can I interact with real Chinese now, before my skills are "good enough"?

The usual answer is to stay in class and talk to your teacher in English. That's the slow road. The faster road is to spend as much time as possible inside Chinese, working with real content even while your skills are still weak. This is exactly where AI helps.

First, build a minimal core. Learn the most basic Chinese that still lets you express almost anything in a simple way. (I've written a separate article on designing this kind of minimal language and using AI to practice it.)

Next, generate real content at your level. Use AI to produce content for you — but real content, from the real world, about things you actually care about. For example, I'll ask:

Generate an article about recent news in Chinese for a learner.
Use simple sentences and everyday spoken language.
Try to use the vocabulary I provide.

(I keep my vocabulary in a separate file and paste it in.) I use DeepSeek to generate these articles.

Then, interact by voice. I paste the article into Doubao, where I have a bot set up to use simple but real Chinese. In voice mode, I ask it to read the first paragraph aloud. If I don't understand, I ask it to reread the first sentence. For any word I don't know, I ask what it means — and it explains in Chinese. Sometimes I ask for the English, which is fine, but I try not to lean on it. The goal is to stay inside Chinese as much as possible while keeping everything understandable, and to actually use what I learn: build a sentence with the new word, check that I understand how it works.

Once I can get through the whole article this way, I listen to it again, paragraph by paragraph. Then I ask the bot to list the words I asked about and add them to my review file.

Here's the key trick: I send that list along with my request for the next article and ask DeepSeek to reuse those words. This way I review old vocabulary naturally, inside new content, instead of drilling static flashcards. You feel like you're moving forward — reading something new every time — while still reinforcing the old.

Keeping vocabulary useful

Keep only genuinely useful words. I ask the AI to rate how useful a word is from 0 to 100, and I ignore anything below 70. There's no point memorizing words you'll rarely meet.

On my list, I also track how familiar I am with each word, again from 0 to 100:

绕开 (70)
理由 (30)

A low number means I barely know it; a high number means I've nearly got it. This tells the AI which words to keep working into new articles.

I also ask the AI to group vocabulary so it's easier to remember:

Help me break down the highly useful vocabulary and phrases
in the following text into categories as a nested list.

And whenever something is too hard, I ask Doubao to slow down and rephrase it more simply. Paraphrasing into the simplest possible form is the single most important skill when learning a language — for the AI and for you.

Working with real content

The same method works on anything real: a documentary, a blog post, a movie.

  1. Take the subtitles or text.
  2. Send them to Doubao and ask it to read sentence by sentence.
  3. Ask questions, practice, and collect new vocabulary (only words rated above 70).
  4. Ask it to read the whole thing again, until it feels familiar.
  5. Finally, watch or read the original.

By the time you reach the original, you've already met the language several times — so the "unreachable" native content becomes reachable.

Summary

  • Core argument: Use AI immediately to access real Chinese content; don't wait for “good enough” skills.
    • Problem: Four Chinese skills don't reinforce each other.
      • Writing: high effort, low payoff.
      • Reading: written vs. spoken are different.
      • Speaking: needs perfect pronunciation first.
      • Listening: hardest to reach, most important.
    • Solution: AI-powered workflow.
      • Build a minimal core first.
      • Generate real content at your level (DeepSeek).
      • Practice by voice (Doubao), stay in Chinese.
      • Reuse vocabulary across new articles.
    • Vocabulary rule: Keep only useful words (rating >70).
      • Track familiarity (0–100) for each word.
      • AI groups words into categories.
    • Real content method: Subtitles → AI reads sentence by sentence → practice → review → watch original.
    • Result: Native content becomes reachable; fluency accelerates.

Conclusion

The sooner you learn to stay inside Chinese and interact with real Chinese content, the faster you'll learn the language.

Modern AI tools let you stay inside the language almost 100% of the time, even before your skills feel ready. That's the opportunity — so use it.
