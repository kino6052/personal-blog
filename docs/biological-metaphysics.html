Biological Metaphysics

Molecules that don't explain anything

In contemporary scientific popularization, there is a pervasive and epistemologically flawed tendency to reduce complex human behavior to the mere secretion of neurotransmitters. Modern literature frequently asserts that an individual engages in physical exertion to receive a "reward" of dopamine or endorphins. From a rigorous methodological standpoint, this is an undisciplined approach. It substitutes objective causality with chemical nomenclature, yielding an explanation that possesses essentially zero predictive power. To understand human behavior, we must not begin with the microscopic machinery, but rather with the organism’s objective interaction with its material environment. When we elevate specific chemicals to the status of primary motivators, we commit an error of idealism: we multiply entities without necessity, conjuring an internal "manager" that supposedly distributes chemical rewards to the body. This is a severe deviation from empirical discipline. Let us examine the mechanics of systematic physical exertion. In reality, the act of exercising is not inherently pleasurable; it is the application of acute physiological stress. The organism is placed under immense pressure and forced to expend energy near its limits. How, then, does the organism determine that this stress is ultimately beneficial? It does not require a chemical messenger to formulate this conclusion. The answer lies in the fundamental physiological principle of tension and release. During exertion, the organism resists and endures. When the labor ceases, the intense pressure is removed. The profound sense of well-being that follows is simply the organism’s natural physiological compensation for having survived the stress. It is the contrast between strain and homeostasis. Even the most fundamental bodily functions—such as physiological excretions—operate on this exact principle: the accumulation of physical tension and the profound relief of its resolution. The relief is the reward itself; no intermediate chemical "ghost" is required to explain the essence of the experience. The universal governing law here is adaptation to systematic stress. When an organism is subjected to friction, it invariably adapts. This mechanism is entirely objective and agnostic to the nature of the stimulus. We observe this clearly in detrimental habits, such as the inhalation of tobacco smoke. The introduction of toxic substances initiates severe physiological stress, yet the organism systematically adapts to this friction as well. Over time, it builds a tolerance, and the cessation of the craving—the removal of the stressor—becomes integrated into the individual's routine as a false sense of relief. This identical law of adaptation governs higher intellectual labor. When one undertakes the study of advanced mathematics—for instance, wrestling with the combinatorial synchronization of complex frameworks or attempting to formalize rigorous proofs—the initial experience is one of acute cognitive strain. The brain does not naturally "enjoy" this intense expenditure of analytical energy. However, through systematic application of this intellectual stress, the cognitive apparatus adapts. The friction of the unknown gives way to structural skill and structural mastery. Do neurotransmitters play a role in these processes? Indisputably. However, they belong in the final chapters of our analysis, as the mechanical details of the nervous system’s plumbing. They describe the how of the biological machinery, not the substance of the organism's lived experience. Therefore, when newly published texts claim to reveal "groundbreaking" science regarding how physical or mental labor alters brain chemistry, they present nothing fundamentally new. The fact that the organism adapts to applied stress—whether it be physical culture or rigorous mathematical study—is a foundational law of biology. To obscure the profound material reality of human effort and adaptation behind a veil of chemical reductionism is to abandon the discipline of true empirical science.
