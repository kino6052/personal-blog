Part 1 - Chapter 1 - Prior to Understanding

In part one of this book, I want to share my experience of building a fundamental understanding of reality from scratch. It was a system born out of necessity, assembled piece by piece through deep philosophical introspection. While it has its flaws, I believe it offers an essential key to unraveling the quiet confusion so many of us live in without realizing it. Before I can explain how I arrived at this understanding, I must explain why it became necessary in the first place. It began as an attempt to assemble the messy puzzle of misconceptions I found myself trapped in when I was younger—a deep, disorienting confusion about life, myself, and the world around me.

When my family and I moved to the United States from Russia, my brother and I were around twenty years old. We were young, full of energy, possessed a modest amount of talent, and were thoroughly filled with attractive misconceptions. We were the ideal candidates for entering the "meat grinder of dreams" that is America. We expected nothing less than to become the next world-sensation rock band that everyone would be talking about. We were not against the societal myths handed to us; they were far too alluring to question. In our minds, we were simply covert celebrities whose time had not quite arrived yet.

Then, reality hit. Living in an English-speaking country, you quickly realize that the English you learned in school is practically useless. You have to discard everything and start from scratch, an experience that is profoundly humbling. Just when you thought you were interesting and capable, you suddenly find yourself speaking like a quiet, muffled child. My Russian cadence did not register with American speakers, and my listening comprehension was so poor that whole conversations passed over me like noise. Without fluent language, making meaningful connections became almost impossible. All our friends were on the other side of the globe, and my brother and I were entirely on our own. Because we were too old for high school and not yet state residents to afford college, our days were spent volunteering at a food pantry, taking basic ESL classes, and staying home.

The first year in the U.S. was one of the hardest years of my life. What we considered normal back home required intense planning or was viewed with suspicion. I vividly remember when my dad was teaching my brother and me how to drive in an empty mall parking lot, something completely ordinary and harmless in Russia. Someone called the police. When the patrol car pulled up, we were terrified and confused. We did not want conflict, so we put our hands on the wheel, answered their questions nervously, and tried to understand why a trivial family moment required police intervention. It was humiliating and frightening, forcing us to second-guess our every move. Assimilating was not just difficult; it was teeth-grindingly difficult. It took five to six years of university study and relentless effort to finally speak and understand the language well.

As naive as our expectations for immigration were, our music ambitions were even more foolish. My brother and I loved creating music. We had our songs, crafted a demo album, designed the artwork, and handed it out to friends. We secretly hoped for a lazy miracle—that someone would discover our song online and make us famous overnight. But when we gave out our demos, nothing followed. Nobody really liked it that much, and polite silence met our efforts. The reality was that we lacked the understanding of what doing music professionally actually entails. I did not care for performing; live shows and promotion felt like unglamorous, draining work that quickly killed our motivation. What I loved was the pure, creative act of making music. I naively assumed that creating something nice was the golden goose and that the rest would happen naturally. In hindsight, I am deeply grateful we never made it into the music industry. It is a meat grinder that squeezes people dry, driven by an ideology that encourages musicians to spin their wheels until they drop dead. But at the time, the quiet rejection proved just how easily we had been manipulated by our own unexamined fantasies.

Why were these collisions so painful? Because my brother and I had spent our lives inside a giant monocultural bubble. Whether it is Russia, the United States, or China, massive monocultural environments breed an illusion of universality. You grow up believing that the way people live, eat, talk, budget, and interact in your environment is simply how the world works. In post-Soviet Russia, life could be unglamorous and low-cost, grounded by a personal garden in the suburbs as a tangible safety net. In contrast, precarious American capitalism was tightly budgeted, fast-paced, and highly individualistic, where every minor misstep carried financial consequences. When you step out of one bubble and into another, you are forced to relearn everything from scratch. It is no coincidence that early philosophy flourished in multicultural trade centers like Miletus, where thinkers like Thales lived. When diverse cultures collide, people are forced to realize that their local customs are not universal truths.

From birth, most of us are indoctrinated into a web of unearned assumptions. We absorb romanticized metrics of success fed to us by media and movies, alongside unquestioned metaphysical beliefs that reality consists of raw, dead matter evolving by chance and that consciousness is merely a secondary byproduct of brain circuitry. We carry these ideas around as if they were proven facts, yet we have never tested them, earned them, or questioned their foundation.

The mess in your concepts inevitably manifests as a mess in your life. If you build your existence on a shaky foundation of unexamined assumptions, the structure will eventually collapse under the weight of reality. For me, that collapse happened on a dark, heavy afternoon. My family and I were visiting family friends who had two young daughters. On the way there, I got into a bitter quarrel with my parents. I stopped speaking to them entirely, which left them deeply worried. In my head, a dark, desperate logic was taking over: if moving to America was such a disappointing waste, then every dream is an illusion, life is just pain, and none of it is real. I thought to myself that I would simply kill myself that day.

Later that night, after drinking a massive amount of vodka to numb the pain, I fell into a drunken sleep. When I woke up the next morning, I felt an overwhelming sense of misery, guilt, and terror. I felt as though I had physically and spiritually damaged myself and my relationships. I was horrified that I had been on the verge of making an unalterable, permanent decision over what was ultimately a temporary crisis. I cried on the phone, begging forgiveness from the friends we had visited. That morning was my rock bottom, but it was also my moment of radical clarity. I realized that living on unexamined assumptions is not harmless—it is dangerous. I told myself that if I did not unravel my life's yarn right now, I would keep making the stupidest mistakes that would leave me miserable and dead.

Admitting that you are confused, lost, or unhappy is not a sign of weakness. It is the most honest and valuable thing a human being can do. It is the emergency brake that forces you to stop running on autopilot, pause, and inspect the machine. When facing life's disorientation, people usually turn to two familiar paths: blind faith, by adopting a pre-packaged set of spiritual answers, or material science, by looking to physical measurements to explain human meaning—a domain science was never designed to solve.

There is a third path that most people neglect: philosophy. Modern culture often dismisses philosophy as an impotent art of asking endless, impractical questions. But philosophy is not an academic luxury; it is a survival discipline. It is the systematic practice of demanding what we can actually be certain of, what assumptions we are making without knowing it, and how we know what is real. As Socrates famously declared, an unexamined life is not worth living. To fix the mess in my life, I had to fix the mess in my concepts. I had to tear down the unearned assumptions handed to me by society and start building a new foundation from the ground up—an epistemological approach that begins not with assumptions about dead matter, but with what is directly perceived by the mind. In the next chapter, we begin that process of unraveling.
