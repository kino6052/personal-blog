If the "Why?" question is the most fundamental inquiry we can make, then the meaning of life is the ultimate "Why?" question. What is the primary purpose of life as an activity? What are we ultimately striving to achieve?

To answer this, I begin with a simple thought experiment: imagine that every single one of your needs and wants is completely, 100% fulfilled.

The keyword here is completely. It is surprisingly difficult to picture because our lives are saturated with endless micro-needs. You would not need to breathe, to move, to look around, or to think. You would be entirely stationary, both on the outside and on the inside. In that state of total fulfillment, why would you ever bother to act?

You wouldn't. Every action, every movement, and every desire is an attempt to resolve an imbalance—a need or a want arising from our inherent incompleteness as creatures within the phenomenal world.

Life as a physical activity has one primary goal: completion—the continuous maintenance of homeostasis. Unlike inorganic matter, living organisms carry informational blueprints that dictate their form, behavior, and repair mechanisms. From a single cell regulating its internal chemistry to an entire civilization building infrastructure, every living system struggles continuously to maintain stability against entropy and achieve complete rest.

Yet, this earthly goal is a logical impossibility. Because we exist within time and space, our wants are often distorted interpretations of our true needs, leading us to chase illusions that cause grief and dissatisfaction. Worse still, even if we managed to balance our needs temporarily, we hit an absolute wall: death. Personal mortality, the death of loved ones, and the eventual decay of species render all purely material striving an incomplete, temporary stir. The phenomenal world cannot supply a final, permanent rest; every temporary resolution merely generates new tensions. We are like Sisyphus endlessly pushing a stone uphill, or a donkey chasing a dangling carrot.

The Unintelligence of Materialism vs. The Transcendent Path

When I lived inside a materialist reference frame, death seemed like a simple, biological light switch—a guaranteed exit into non-existence. I treated life as something to cruise through while seeking immediate pleasures, using the inevitability of death as a convenient excuse for laziness, cowardice, and egoism.

However, once I shifted my reference frame to the Mind-first model, that naive comfort dissolved. If consciousness and the Sustainer Mind are foundational, death is not a guaranteed eraser. As Shakespeare’s Hamlet observed: "To sleep, perchance to dream: ay, there's the rub; for in that sleep of death what dreams may come?" In a Mind-first reality, death must be taken with absolute seriousness.

This realization brings us back to our definition of intelligence from Chapter 4.

Recall that physical objects resolve conflict greedily—taking the immediate, path-of-least-resistance vector to local rest, like a falling rock or colliding particles. Intelligence, by contrast, is defined by indirectness and sacrifice: accepting a short-term cost or detour to achieve a deeper, more complete resolution later.

In ordinary earthly life, sacrificing immediate pleasure does not always guarantee a reward; a business venture may fail, or a planned compromise may yield nothing. Yet, when a human being dismisses the possibility of a transcendent escape from the phenomenal realm, they are acting fundamentally unintelligently. They are doing the exact same thing as a rock: greedily chasing immediate, local rest within a system where permanent fulfillment is mathematically impossible.

To cling strictly to the phenomenal loop is to accept a 0% chance of ultimate fulfillment. To sacrifice immediate, greedy worldly desires and seek a transcendent path is the only rational, intelligent choice available to a conscious mind—it is the only path where the probability of true completion is greater than zero.

Two Paths of Transcendence: Buddhism vs. Christianity

Humanity has long felt this deep existential tension, giving rise to various religious traditions seeking an escape from decay and death.

Many mythological systems—such as those of the Greeks, Romans, or Norse—merely projected phenomenal incompleteness onto their gods. Their deities were physically powerful but emotionally volatile, subject to the same petty conflicts and temporal limits as humans.

Only a few traditions offer a radical, complete exit from phenomenal incompleteness. The two greatest contenders are Buddhism and Christianity.

Buddhism correctly diagnoses the core problem: that clinging to desires within an impermanent world is the root of all suffering. However, its proposed method is essentially an algorithm of dissolution. Buddhism attempts to eliminate suffering by dissolving the very self that searches for completeness. Despite its peaceful verbal coating, it is effectively a spiritual self-annihilation—stopping the friction of life by dismantling the engine of consciousness.

Christianity takes a radically different and far more rational approach. It does not demand the destruction of the self; it promises its redemption and ultimate completion. It asserts that fulfillment is real, personal, and attainable because the soul was created for eternal relationship with the Sustainer Mind.

The Incarnation and Divine Paradoxes (Dialecticisms)

In Christianity, the human being is not a marginal accident in a cold universe, but a central participant in a cosmic reality. The universe was brought into being as the environment for conscious creatures endowed with free decision-making—a gift so profound that its misuse became the source of our deep earthly misery.

By misusing our free will, humanity fractured its alignment with God, altering our nature and binding us to mortality, corruption, and confusion. The primary conflict of human existence is the problem of good and evil—the gap between our broken, greedy desires and the ultimate purity of God.

Because we are finite and trapped inside the phenomenal loop, we cannot fix this fracture on our own terms. Christianity reveals that the resolution comes entirely from God, who bridges the gap between Heaven and Earth through the Incarnation.

When the Divine enters the material display, human logic is completely turned upside down. This produces what I call dialecticisms—profound divine paradoxes that intentionally shatter our earthly, "greedy" assumptions about power, status, and religion:

  • The Sovereign Creator of the universe is born as a fragile infant in a dirty feeding trough.
  • A humble Virgin gives birth to the Eternal Word.
  • The self-righteous, religious elite are condemned, while outcast tax collectors and prostitutes find justification.
  • It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the Kingdom of God.
  • The very first person promised entry into Paradise by Christ is a condemned thief dying on an adjacent cross.

The ultimate dialecticism occurred on Golgotha: God incarnate was betrayed, mocked, and murdered by the very people chosen to preserve His truth. Human logic looks at the Crucifixion and sees absolute failure and horror. Yet, through sacrificial love, that catastrophe became the ultimate resolution—destroying the power of death and offering forgiveness to the very creatures who committed the crime.

Some might point to eastern traditions, asking whether figures like Krishna represent a similar concept—an avatar descending to guide souls. But Krishna does not offer a true escape from the incomplete phenomenal cycles, nor does he offer ultimate sacrificial love. Did Krishna willingly lay down his life for us? Did he suffer human torture, die, and resurrect in history to conquer death? Did he explain the deep, cosmic necessity of human suffering and redemption?

Christ's sacrifice is unique. God did not observe our suffering from a comfortable distance; He entered the human meat grinder, endured its worst horrors, and transformed suffering into the very vehicle of our liberation.

Faith as Radical Softness vs. Legalistic Point-Counting

To understand Christianity through a clean, uncorrupted lens, we must reject the trap of legalistic religion.

Many people treat faith like a corporate point-collection game. They view righteousness as a rigid, brittle checklist: If I perform these external rituals, avoid those specific actions, and accumulate enough moral points, God owes me a reward.

This legalistic mindset is a hardened, reified disaster. It breeds spiritual pride, causes us to compare ourselves obsessively to others, and turns religion into a hypocritical display. It is the exact mindset of the Pharisees that Christ explicitly condemned. Conversely, hiding behind a lazy passivity—claiming "God will handle everything" as an excuse to avoid personal growth—is equally corrupt.

True faith is not a rigid code; it is radically soft.

Bringing back our software metaphor from the Introduction, legalism is like a hard-coded, brittle system that breaks the moment real-world complexity hits. True faith is an open, humble, and resilient posture of the heart—a deep, trusting relationship between the finite mind and the Sustainer Mind. It cannot be measured by external scorecards because God looks directly at the soul.

Through this lens, the suffering and trials we face in life take on a profound, pedagogical meaning. Suffering is not meaningless physical noise; it is the fire that burns away our false reliance on worldly illusions. It humbles our pride, exposes our weakness, and forces us to stop trying to solve life through greedy, material means. It teaches us that our only true anchor is God.

The Ultimate Anchor

When the Mind-first reference frame meets Christian faith, the foundational "Why?" of human existence receives a complete, coherent answer.

We are not random biological accidents trapped in a dead, unthinking universe. We are conscious spirits navigating a temporary, patterned world of change designed to train our free will, humble our ego, and direct our hearts toward eternal rest in God.

This faith does not offer a cheap, easy life, but it offers something infinitely greater: a quiet, unbreakable peace of mind. It assures us that our struggles are not in vain, that suffering has purpose, and that ultimate love and justice await beyond the phenomenal horizon.

With our theoretical foundation set in Part 1 and our spiritual anchor secured here in Chapter 5, we are now ready to return to the phenomenal world. In the next chapter, we will re-examine the physical sciences—mathematics, physics, biology, and computer science—through this exact empirical, meaning-centered lens.
