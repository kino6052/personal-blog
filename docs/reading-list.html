Reading Against Myself

A checklist of the major works I need to be in honest dialogue with other fundamental thinkers

Home Page

I have read widely in the history of philosophy — Plato, Aristotle, Aquinas, Descartes and others — but the framework I write from rests mainly on two books: Berkeley's Principles of Human Knowledge and Kant's Critique of Pure Reason. Recently I added Schopenhauer's On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason and Ernst Mach.

A framework that rests on a few pillars has a specific weakness, however widely its author has read. I can't always tell which of my conclusions are discoveries and which are rediscoveries. And I am most likely to miss the strongest arguments against my own starting points, because they come from thinkers who took the same tools further, or in another direction, than I did.

So this list has three purposes:

  1. The critics. Thinkers who used my own tools — the same razor I take from Berkeley — against my conclusions.
  2. The ancestors. Thinkers who reached my ideas before me, so I can cite and build on them instead of rederiving them.
  3. The traditions I make claims about. Buddhism, Chinese thought, physics: I write about all of them, so I should read them at the source.

Some entries are books I have read before, for other reasons. They are here because I now need them to answer a specific question. Each entry says where it touches something I have written. Entries marked ★ come first.

Notes on my recent reading

Schopenhauer agrees with my mechanism but not my conclusion. In The World as Will and Representation, the will never reaches rest. Satisfaction is only a short break from pain, and life "swings like a pendulum" between pain and boredom (§57). That is nearly my essay Why Intelligence Can Never Rest. But his way out is to deny the will — the same dissolution of the self I reject in Buddhism. Our sharpest disagreement is in Book 4, not in the Fourfold Root.

Mach is not just a weaker Berkeley. He is Berkeley without the perceiving spirit and without God. His "elements" are neither mental nor physical, and he writes that "the ego cannot be saved." That is Hume's move, and it is the biggest threat to my foundation: if Berkeley's razor removes material substance, what stops it from removing mental substance too?

Hegel deserves a fairer target. "Thesis–antithesis–synthesis" is Fichte's vocabulary, popularized by Chalybäus. Hegel rarely used it, and in the Preface to the Phenomenology he dismisses formulaic triads as a "lifeless schema." Chapter 4 of my book credits him with the triad. I may still reject Hegel, but the critique should hit what he actually wrote.

1. The critics of my foundation

  • ★ David Hume — A Treatise of Human Nature, 1.4.6 "Of personal identity," and An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, §7 "Of the idea of necessary connexion." He turns Berkeley's razor on the self and on causal necessity. That challenges both my anchor in the certainty of the "I" and my claim that "every cause is a conflict" is known a priori.

  • ★ George Berkeley — Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous, the third dialogue. Hylas raises exactly the objection above, and Philonous answers that we have a "notion" of spirit. I need to decide whether that answer satisfies me.

  • ★ Immanuel Kant — the Paralogisms of Pure Reason, the Refutation of Idealism (B274), and the Prolegomena, §13. Kant's direct reply to Berkeley by name, and to "the I is the one certain substance." I use Kant against the noumenon; here he uses himself against me.

  • Plato — Theaetetus. The original examination of "knowledge is perception," and Socrates's case against it.

  • Friedrich Nietzsche — Beyond Good and Evil, Part 1, especially §16–17. "A thought comes when 'it' wants": the "I" in "I think" as a habit of grammar. My own anti-reification tool, pointed at my cogito.

  • G. W. F. Hegel — Phenomenology of Spirit, the Preface and chapter 1, "Sense-Certainty." He argues that bare "this, here, now" perception is the emptiest kind of knowledge. The fairest test of whether Hegel is sophistry.

  • Wilfrid Sellars — "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind," in the edition with Robert Brandom's study guide. The "Myth of the Given": perception can't ground knowledge unless it is already conceptual.

  • ★ W. V. O. Quine — "On What There Is" and "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." The first is the standard modern account of when a theory is committed to an entity — my "ghost" question made formal. The second attacks the analytic/synthetic distinction my a priori/a posteriori split depends on.

  • ★ Ludwig Wittgenstein — Philosophical Investigations, §65–67 (family resemblance as an alternative to essence) and §243–315 (the private language argument). The deepest challenge to essence-seeking and to building meaning out of private experience.

2. The ancestors of my ideas

  • Heraclitus and Parmenides — the fragments, in Robin Waterfield's The First Philosophers. The title of my book paraphrases Heraclitus; Parmenides argued that change is unreal.

  • Aristotle — Physics I–III, Categories 1–5, and Metaphysics VII. "The rule never stalls" in What Are Prime Numbers? is his potential infinity. Categories read against Metaphysics VII also untangles substance and essence, two words I use in opposite senses in different essays.

  • ★ Augustine — Confessions, I.1 and Book XI. "Our heart is restless until it rests in You" is Chapter 5 in one sentence. Book XI calls time a "distension of the mind" — that is Chapter 4.

  • Al-Ghazali — The Incoherence of the Philosophers (the 17th discussion) and Deliverance from Error. Fire doesn't cause cotton to burn; God coordinates the two events. That is my brain/mind coordination, six centuries before Berkeley. Deliverance moves from crisis to doubt to faith, as my book does.

  • Nicolas Malebranche — Dialogues on Metaphysics and on Religion. Occasionalism, and Berkeley's immediate source.

  • G. W. Leibniz — Monadology. Where the principle of sufficient reason starts; his pre-established harmony is my VR-engine image.

  • Baruch Spinoza — Ethics, Parts II–III. His conatus says everything strives to persist — the opposite of "everything wills rest." His parallelism of mind and body is the "two frames of reference" in my Manifesto of Decoupled Science.

  • ★ Arthur Schopenhauer — The World as Will and Representation, vol. 1, Book 4, and the appendix "Criticism of the Kantian Philosophy." The continuation of the Fourfold Root, and the place where my disagreement with him is sharpest (see the note above).

  • Sigmund Freud — Beyond the Pleasure Principle. The death drive as an urge to return to an earlier, inorganic rest. With it I can place myself among four positions on what everything wants:

    • to persist (Spinoza);
    • to strive without end (Schopenhauer);
    • to return to rest (Aristotle's natural places, Freud);
    • to rest only in God (Augustine — and me).

  • Blaise Pascal — Pensées, the wager fragment. My "0% versus ε" argument in Change. I need to know the many-gods objection; my comparison of Christianity and Buddhism is in effect my answer to it.

  • Charles Sanders Peirce — "The Fixation of Belief" and "How to Make Our Ideas Clear." The pragmatic maxim is my grounding test, published in 1878.

  • Alfred North Whitehead — Science and the Modern World, chapter 3, and The Concept of Nature, chapter 2. His "fallacy of misplaced concreteness" is my reification; his "bifurcation of nature" is my critique of materialism.

  • Henri Bergson — "An Introduction to Metaphysics" and Time and Free Will. The main philosopher of change: lived duration comes before time treated as space.

3. Natural philosophy: Newton, Einstein, and their debates

  • Galileo Galilei — The Assayer (excerpts). Colors and tastes "reside only in consciousness," and the book of nature is "written in the language of mathematics." Where the split I reject begins.

  • Isaac Newton — Principia: the Scholium on absolute space and time, the "Rules of Reasoning in Philosophy" (Book III), and the General Scholium. Absolute space and time as containers are what Chapter 4 opposes. Rule 1, "admit no more causes than are true and sufficient," is my anti-ghost principle. "Hypotheses non fingo" is my "science describes how, not why."

  • ★ Leibniz and Samuel Clarke — The Leibniz–Clarke Correspondence. Leibniz argues space and time are relations; Clarke, speaking for Newton, defends absolute space as God's "sensorium." The classic debate behind my claim that space and time are relational.

  • George Berkeley — De Motu, and Principles, §110–117. Berkeley against Newton: forces are mathematical tools, not real entities — my "force is a label" in Change.

  • Ernst Mach — The Science of Mechanics, chapter 2, the section on Newton's views of time, space and motion. Mach's strongest book; his critique of Newton's bucket argument was a starting point Einstein credited.

  • ★ Albert Einstein — "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies" (1905), §1, and Relativity: The Special and the General Theory, chapter 8. He gets rid of absolute simultaneity by asking which observation, with clocks and light signals, it could correspond to. The most successful case of grounding a concept in perception in the history of physics.

  • ★ Albert Einstein — "Geometry and Experience" and "Autobiographical Notes" (in Schilpp, ed., Albert Einstein: Philosopher-Scientist). "As far as the laws of mathematics refer to reality, they are not certain" bears on my a priori/a posteriori split. The Notes credit Hume and Mach, then explain why he became a realist.

  • Werner Heisenberg — Physics and Beyond, the chapter on his 1926 conversation with Einstein. Heisenberg built quantum mechanics from observable quantities only — my rule. Einstein answered that "it is the theory which decides what we can observe." The strongest objection to perception-first thinking ever made by a physicist.

  • Niels Bohr — "Discussion with Einstein on Epistemological Problems in Atomic Physics" (also in the Schilpp volume). The realism debate at its peak.

  • Henri Poincaré — Science and Hypothesis. Geometry as convention: support for "mathematics is rules," and an influence on Einstein.

4. Philosophy of science

  • Pierre Duhem — The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory. A working physicist arguing that physics describes but does not explain — Chapter 6.

  • Karl Popper — Conjectures and Refutations, chapters 1, 3 and 6. Chapter 6 is "A Note on Berkeley as Precursor of Mach and Einstein." Chapter 1 is my critique of Domain-Driven Design: unfalsifiable theories explaining away every failure.

  • Thomas Kuhn — The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. The Illusion of Progress in mature form.

  • Imre Lakatos — "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes." How a theory's protected core survives failure — the "it wasn't done correctly" defense.

  • Rudolf Carnap — The Logical Structure of the World (the Aufbau). The most serious attempt to build everything from elementary experiences — a real test of my claim that grounding in perception is sufficient.

  • Nelson Goodman — The Structure of Appearance. Why the Aufbau stalled. The most useful lesson in this list for Empirical Software.

  • ★ Bas van Fraassen — The Scientific Image and The Empirical Stance. The best modern defense of my view on atoms: accept theories as empirically adequate, stay agnostic about what can't be observed.

  • David Lindley — Boltzmann's Atom. The Mach–Boltzmann dispute over atoms is the historical test of "if you can't perceive it, it's a ghost," and Mach is usually judged to have lost.

5. Mind and consciousness

  • Edmund Husserl — Cartesian Meditations, then The Crisis of European Sciences. Phenomenology is a disciplined version of "begin with what is given to consciousness." The Crisis blames Galileo for hiding the lived world, close to my science manifesto.

  • Thomas Nagel — "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"

  • David Chalmers — "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness." The source of the "hard problem" I keep citing.

  • Daniel Dennett — "Quining Qualia." The strongest materialist reply, to be read as the best case against me.

  • John Foster — A World for Us: The Case for Phenomenalistic Idealism. A rigorous modern defense of Berkeley-style idealism with God ordering experience. The closest ally I know of.

6. Ethics and the good life

  • Aristotle — Nicomachean Ethics. The baseline account of the good life my questionnaire asks about.

  • Arthur Schopenhauer — On the Basis of Morality. Compassion rather than duty as the foundation of ethics, argued against Kant. That is Morality Is Not Make-Believe, and the natural next book after the Fourfold Root.

  • Mencius — 《孟子·公孙丑上》, 2A6: the child at the well and the "four sprouts." Inborn compassion that can be grown or stunted — the same structure as my math-intuition analogy.

  • Søren Kierkegaard — The Sickness unto Death. Despair and faith, from a fellow critic of Hegel's systems.

  • Fyodor Dostoevsky — "The Grand Inquisitor," The Brothers Karamazov, Book V, chapter 5. People trading freedom for bread and comfort — The Script of Freedom.

7. Traditions I make claims about

  • Bhikkhu Bodhi, ed. — In the Buddha's Words. I call Buddhism self-annihilation, but the Buddha explicitly rejected annihilationism as a wrong view. I should meet that before arguing against it.

  • Nāgārjuna — Mūlamadhyamakakārikā. The most radical anti-reification thinker there is: nothing has inherent existence.

  • Vasubandhu — Twenty Verses. Consciousness-only idealism, argued with the same dream argument I use.

  • Laozi — 《道德经》, especially chapter 16: "归根曰静," returning to the root is called stillness.

  • Zhuangzi — 《齐物论》, chapter 2, ending with the butterfly dream I cite in my questionnaire.

  • Gongsun Long — 《白马论》, the "White Horse" dialogue. A debate about names and reification.

  • Zhu Xi and Lü Zuqian — 《近思录》, Reflections on Things at Hand. Zhu Xi's 理 (principle) is a single unifying principle — a test for my claim in The Architecture of Chaos that Chinese thought never looked for one.

  • Vladimir Solovyov — «Кризис западной философии (против позитивистов)», The Crisis of Western Philosophy: Against the Positivists. A Christian critique of positivism from the Russian tradition.

8. Mathematics and language

  • Gottlob Frege — The Foundations of Arithmetic. The strongest attack on empiricist views of number; my primes essay should survive it.

  • David Hilbert — "On the Infinite." Mathematics as a game with rules, held to the finitary standpoint.

  • L. E. J. Brouwer — "Intuitionism and Formalism." Mathematics as only what a process can construct. My primes essay sits between him and Hilbert.

  • George Lakoff — Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things. Supports my view that classification is experiential, not taxonomic.

  • George Lakoff and Mark Johnson — Metaphors We Live By. Argues that all abstract thought rests on metaphor — a direct challenge to The Analogy Trap.

  • Guy Deutscher — Through the Language Glass. A careful, critical look at "language shapes thought," the thesis of my ОНТОЛОГИЯ ЯЗЫКА.

  • Umberto Eco — The Search for the Perfect Language. The history of invented philosophical languages that 简单话 belongs to.

9. Philosophy of programming

The same rule applies here as with Hegel: I criticize these works often, so I should criticize what they actually say.

The sources of what I critique

  • ★ Robert C. Martin — "Design Principles and Design Patterns" (2000), then Clean Architecture. The paper is where the SOLID principles first appear together. In Clean Architecture the Single Responsibility Principle becomes "responsible to one, and only one, actor" — the stakeholder definition I attack in SOLID Is Aristotelian Metaphysics. The same book also argues "The Database Is a Detail" and "Frameworks Are Details," and its "Screaming Architecture" chapter is my Decoupling Code from File Structure. He is closer to me than I usually admit.

  • Robert C. Martin — Clean Code. The book most of my profession learned "clean" from, and the one I call aesthetic rather than epistemic.

  • Bertrand Meyer — Object-Oriented Software Construction, and Barbara Liskov and Jeannette Wing — "A Behavioral Notion of Subtyping." Where the Open-Closed and Liskov Substitution principles actually come from, before they were packed into an acronym.

  • ★ Eric Evans — Domain-Driven Design: chapter 2 on the ubiquitous language, chapters 5–6 on entities, aggregates and repositories, and Part IV on bounded contexts. I call the domain "the organization's model of its own bureaucracy." This is the book that claim has to survive.

  • Martin Fowler — Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture. The origin of the Repository, the Service Layer and the Data Mapper — the "ghosts" in The Labyrinth of Fake Entities. Add his short essay "Anemic Domain Model": by its standard, my plain data and pure functions are an anti-pattern.

  • Gamma, Helm, Johnson and Vlissides — Design Patterns, chapter 1. The zoo of named patterns I call reified, and the two principles from it I keep anyway: program to an interface, favor composition over inheritance.

  • Alan Kay — "The Early History of Smalltalk." What object-oriented programming meant before it meant class hierarchies: messages, not things.

Allies, ancestors and rivals

  • ★ Fred Brooks — "No Silver Bullet" and "'No Silver Bullet' Refired," in The Mythical Man-Month (anniversary edition). Essence and accident in software, at the source of my Silver Bullet and Context Dependency.

  • ★ Ben Moseley and Peter Marks — "Out of the Tar Pit." Essential versus accidental state, and derived data as accidental state. The closest existing statement of why my essence keeps one fact instead of a fact plus a cached count.

  • David Parnas — "On the Criteria To Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules." Decompose by the decisions likely to change. The strongest rival to my rule of decomposing by what the user perceives.

  • Edsger W. Dijkstra — "On the Role of Scientific Thought" (EWD447). Where "separation of concerns" was coined.

  • Peter Naur — "Programming as Theory Building." The program is a theory in programmers' minds, not the source text — Software Is Made of Ideas, Not Matter.

  • Harold Abelson and Gerald Jay Sussman — Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs. "Programs must be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute." Abstraction barriers done honestly.

  • John Ousterhout — A Philosophy of Software Design, and his published debate with Robert Martin. Deep modules, complexity as dependencies plus obscurity, and a direct argument against Clean Code's small-function advice.

  • Alistair Cockburn — "Hexagonal Architecture." Ports and adapters: the pure core with accidents at the edge, named in 2005.

  • Gary Bernhardt — "Boundaries" (talk). "Functional core, imperative shell" — my Rule of the Pure Core in one phrase.

  • Mark Seemann and Steven van Deursen — Dependency Injection: Principles, Practices, and Patterns. Where the "composition root" I build every app around comes from.

  • Rich Hickey — "Simple Made Easy" and "Are We There Yet?" (talks). The first separates simple from easy; the second builds identity, state and time on Whitehead.

  • Kent Beck — Test-Driven Development: By Example; Martin Fowler — Refactoring. The loop I use for every change to an essence, and the discipline that keeps software soft.

  • Christopher Alexander — Notes on the Synthesis of Form and A Pattern Language, with Richard Gabriel's Patterns of Software. Design as resolving conflicts between form and context — my conflict-resolution model, applied to design — and the architect whose "patterns" programmers borrowed and, by Gabriel's account, misunderstood.

  • Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores — Understanding Computers and Cognition. Heidegger applied to software design, and the easiest way into Heidegger for me.

Order of attack

If I only read ten, in this order:

  1. Hume, Treatise 1.4.6 and Enquiry §7
  2. Berkeley, Three Dialogues, the third dialogue
  3. Kant, the Paralogisms and the Refutation of Idealism
  4. Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Representation, vol. 1, Book 4
  5. Augustine, Confessions I and XI
  6. The Leibniz–Clarke Correspondence
  7. Einstein, the 1905 paper §1, then "Autobiographical Notes"
  8. Quine, "On What There Is" and "Two Dogmas of Empiricism"
  9. Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations §65–67 and §243–315
  10. van Fraassen, The Scientific Image

The first three test my foundation. Schopenhauer and Augustine place my metaphysics of change among existing views. Leibniz–Clarke and Einstein test my claims about space, time and physics. The last three test my grounding criterion against its strongest modern critics.

For programming, four more, in this order: Brooks, "Out of the Tar Pit," Evans, and Martin's Clean Architecture. The first two are where my position comes from; the last two are what it argues against.

An understanding that has never met its strongest critics is not soft. It is only untested.
