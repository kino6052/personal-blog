Part 1 - Chapter 3 - Mind vs Matter

I now want to examine what happens when I choose the mind as the reference frame for my understanding of the world, and see where that decision leads.

If I were to dismiss the world of experience as a total chimera, an arbitrary illusion, I would not bother describing it in detail or trying to form an understanding of it. That radical brand of skepticism is always available as a nuclear option, but why jump to such a hasty conclusion without first taking a serious look at what our actual experience shows us?

The world presented to our senses is full of repeating, law-like patterns. It can be deeply confusing, but it is certainly not random or nonsensical. Day and night alternate with mathematical precision, the seasons cycle, gravity pulls consistently, complex living organisms function, and human societies build intricate machines. Even in wild speculative fiction like The Three-Body Problem, where a star system appears entirely chaotic, the underlying movements still follow predictable physical patterns. My personal thoughts and feelings also exhibit regular patterns, even when I do not fully understand the psychological drivers behind them. The world is saturated with order that can be anticipated and navigated with remarkable accuracy.

This predictability reveals a profound epistemological truth: knowledge is actually possible. We are capable of building mental models that reliably track the world. But how is that correspondence possible? If the world matches the conceptual structures we form in our minds, it must mean that the world itself is fundamentally mind-like. If reality shared no structural correspondence with the nature of mind, we could never form any accurate likeness or understanding of it in our thoughts.

Before I realized I had the choice to adopt mind as my primary reference frame, I naturally took the physical world as the absolute baseline. It seemed obvious. I saw other people growing up alongside me, and I was taught the standard story of modern materialism: that human beings are merely complicated, biological machines assembled through eons of random evolutionary pressures, and that consciousness is just an illusory byproduct generated by brain circuitry. Little did I know that this conventional narrative rests on a fundamental category error.

Behavior is not the same thing as mind, and neither is the brain identical to mind. We learn about brains, neurons, and observable behaviors through experience. The mind, however, is the very container that holds the entirety of experience, alongside the interpretative machinery that turns raw sensation into meaning. Mind is what tells me that an object has a specific color, that two events are causally linked, or that a sequence of sound waves carries intelligible language. Experience conforms to the structures of our understanding. Recognizing that I have a physical body on planet Earth happens entirely within the space of mind. It in no way proves that the physical world would continue to exist if mind were removed from the equation.

To be clear, I do not believe the universe is contingent on my personal, finite mind. That would be an absurd leap into solipsism. The specific model of the world inside my head would certainly vanish if my brain ceased to function, but reality itself is sustained by Mind as such—a universal, continuously aware consciousness in which all appearances subsist.

This realization used to make me uneasy, as if I were wandering off into a dense metaphysical jungle. But along the way, I encountered the work of the Anglo-Irish philosopher George Berkeley, particularly his A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge, which addressed this exact problem with surgical clarity.

Berkeley began by making a crucial distinction between our mind's ability to perceive specific things and its ability to form general abstractions. He pointed out a widespread delusion: people often trick themselves into believing that abstract concepts can exist independently in the real world. As Berkeley famously wrote:

"If we consider it, the idea of matter is a relative idea; it signifies, I know not what: we are told it is a support of accidents, or qualities; but we have no idea of it distinct from the qualities which it is supposed to support. ... If we look ever so narrowly into our own thoughts, we shall find it impossible for us to conceive any kind of existence which is not either a spirit or an idea; and consequently, if any one talks of an unthinking substance, or an unknown support of qualities, he may amuse himself with words, but he talks of nothing at all."

Now, a modern reader might push back here. You likely do not think of "matter" as some vague, abstract philosophical substance without predicates. You think of concrete, modern physics: you point to atoms, quarks, electrons, and subatomic particles. Furthermore, you might appeal to biology and sensory diversity: "Look, we know a cat sees in the dark, and a bee perceives ultraviolet light that humans cannot see. So while human visual appearance isn't absolute, those atoms and quarks still exist out there with some objective form and structure unfolding in space and time, even when no human is looking."

Let us examine that argument carefully, because it contains a subtle but critical flaw.

First, how do we know that a cat or a bee perceives the world differently? We know it by dissecting their visual anatomy, studying their responses, and mapping their sensory apparatuses onto our own scientific instruments—a process that takes place entirely within our mind and sensory experience. Invoking animals does zero work to prove the existence of an unperceived material world. A bee or a cat is simply another perceiving mind receiving a structured flow of ideas. Comparing one organism's perceptual field to another organism's perceptual field merely compares two forms of mental experience; it does not jump outside the realm of perception altogether.

Second, what about those atoms and quarks possessing a "form in space and time" independent of mind?

Ask yourself what space and time actually are. Space is a framework of spatial relations—distance, position, geometry, and shape between boundaries. Time is a framework of temporal relations—succession, duration, before, and after. But a relation inherently requires an act of mind. It requires a conscious framework to relate point A to point B, or moment one to moment two. If you erase every mind from existence, there is no longer anything relating one spatial position to another. To claim that subatomic particles possess "form in space and time" without any mind present is a logical impossibility; it is like claiming a mathematical equation exists when there are no numbers or relationships to evaluate.

At this point, we have to admit a quiet defeat. The very entities we thought we were studying as objective physical reality—atoms, quarks, and unperceived material particles "in themselves"—are not things we can ever perceive, touch, or verify outside of perception. The moment you insist, "Well, particles exist out there beyond experience in a way we can't fully describe," you have fallen right into the exact error of Immanuel Kant. You have invented a ghost—a noumenon—an unprovable entity beyond all possible experience, and then treated it as an established, load-bearing fact. You simply did not realize you were making this mistake.

Why, then, should we blindly accept the unexamined metaphysical assertions of materialist scientists over the crisp, logical framework of Berkeley?

Real science does not study unperceivable, unthinking matter. Real science studies repeatable, mathematical patterns within experience. Science measures how one observable state relates to another observable state. When a physicist writes an equation for a subatomic particle, they are describing regularities in what can be measured and observed through instruments. Science is brilliant at mapping the rules of the game, but the moment a scientist claims that those mathematical rules prove the existence of an unthinking, unperceived material world hiding behind the scenes, they have left empirical science behind and drifted into bad, ungrounded metaphysics.

Berkeley offered a far more honest and grounded path: only mind has true substantiality, and all existing things are ideas sustained by being perceived. Idealism does not deny the reality of the physical world. Whether we call ourselves idealists or materialists, we both observe orderly, predictable laws governing our universe. The difference is that idealism acknowledges that this order is a structured language of ideas maintained by a Sustainer Mind, rather than an unprovable, unperceivable ghost hiding behind the screen.

How, then, do we explain the connection between the physical world and our mental experience—such as the observable fact that brain damage alters perception? Materialism claims the physical brain causes thought, while idealists are often accused of ignoring physical reality. But the relationship is not one of physical cause-and-effect; it is one of coordination.

Think of a modern virtual reality system. When you put on a VR headset and your avatar bumps into a wall, the headset displays a flash, the speakers produce a thud, and the controllers vibrate in your hands. Did the pixels on the display screen physically cause the motor inside the controller to vibrate? No. Did the haptic vibration cause the image on the screen? No. Both sensory events are coordinated simultaneously by an underlying software engine running the simulation.

In our reality, the physical brain and body—along with atoms, neurons, and light waves—are ideas within the perceptual field: images within the display. When a brain injury occurs, perception becomes distorted not because dead matter generates consciousness, but because the Sustainer Mind coordinates the regular rules of experience such that a change in the "brain idea" corresponds to a change in perceived experience. The physical world and the mental world are coordinated through a deeper, unified engine.

Adopting a Mind-first reference frame does not destroy science or sanity. On the contrary, it protects us from two great intellectual traps: the self-contradictions of materialist positivism, and the wild, ungrounded fantasies of speculative metaphysics. It grounds our understanding strictly in what is directly observable and real.

When we inspect this stream of experiences sustained within the Mind, we immediately notice its most undeniable feature: it is never truly static. The world of experience is governed by an endless, restless dynamic of tension, conflict, and resolution seeking rest.

The fundamental pattern of this mind-like reality is not static matter, but continuous change. In the next chapter, we will explore the deep logic of this change and see how it shapes everything from physical systems to human thought.
